Did you know there's a spotify playlist called "Songs About Water"? Well, when I sat down to write this blog, I thought it might put me in the mood to write about water. And then I thought <i> Wait, is this a common experience? This playlist has over 1k saves on Spotify, are all these people writing about water? Why are there so many songs about water? Why doesn't the playlist contain Every Teardrop Is a Waterfall by Coldplay? Do these artists also sing about air?</i> 

No, they don't seem to. Clearly water is important to many people, not least of all musicians. <i> But why? What could be so important about a tasteless, colourless fluid? </i>

Well, for one thing, the sound of water has been shown to alleviate stress so much that outdoor spaces can and should be designed to amplify and project the sound of water widely according to [Brown and Muhar](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/0964056042000284857#d1e281). You might already have found that listening to running water or rain helps you sleep for this reason.

<i> That's strange. What would drive people to universally agree that the sound of running water is relaxing? </i> 

Let's first look out our sense of smell. What we consider to be pleasant smells is driven by evolution; we don't like the smell of feces because it can contain harmful bacteria, pathogens, and parasites ([Biocycle](https://www.biocycle.net/odors-and-human-responses/)) and we must therefore stay away from it. Conversely, humans can survive without water for [only a few days](https://www.bbc.com/future/article/20201016-why-we-cant-survive-without-water), so it makes sense that we would naturally be drawn towards the sound of it. 

However, the sound of water doesn't just indicate its proximity, it can be an indicator of water quality. Water [is a great solvent](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/water-universal-solvent). Therefore, water in the natural environment carries myriad constituents, some of which can be very disagreeable - think of a half eaten deer carcass on the banks of a lake carrying [harmful bacteria and waste products](https://www.everydayhealth.com/food-safety/what-happens-if-you-cook-meat-after-it-has-gone-bad/), or of rain water runoff carrying a turd into a stream. Assuming you can't stop these occurrences, your only protection would be get exposed rarely and in small doses. This is can be achieved by diluting the water to sufficiently low concentrations of the constituents mentioned. In alternative phrasing, you hope that lots of water hasn't been exposed to a little bit of contamination. 

<i> How would this happen naturally? </i>

Rapidly flowing streams carry lots of water (and therefore constituents) from catchment areas. In an established stream in an old catchment, most harmful constituents that may find their way into a stream have been flushed downstream already. What continues to be transported into the stream is only there instantaneously - contaminants are continuously being diluted by water arriving from upstream and flushed downstream. More water generally means more dilution. More water also generally means more noise, and brings us back to explaining why the sound of water is so positively received. 

<i> Well, that sounds good, but I don't think I would trust some of the world's rivers enough to race in them, let alone drink from them. What's the dealio? </i>

Unfortunately, this key feature of rivers - the ability to carry waste downstream (and away) - has been outpaced by population growth and careless dumping practices. Approximately [2.7 billion people lack access to solid waste collection globally](https://www.unep.org/interactives/beat-waste-pollution/), with approximately [1000 rivers accounting for 80% of global annual riverine plastic emissions](https://theoceancleanup.com/sources/). Nowadays, rivers transport millions of tonnes of plastic to the sea. The dilution that used to happen still happens a bit - in Perth we don't see <i> that </i> much plastic in the Swan or at the beach - however, global currents often transport huge quantities of rubbish to the [Great Pacific Garbage Patch](https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/great-pacific-garbage-patch/) or similar. Waste water treatment for this environmental problem includes [scraping floating material from the surface between two barges](https://theoceancleanup.com/oceans/) for collection and disposal onshore, and [collecting material from rover mouths](https://theoceancleanup.com/rivers/) before they enter the ocean and contribute to the problem further.

<i> What about modern drinking water? Do we still just hope for the best? </i>

Often, yes. [In 2022, globally, at least 1.7 billion people use a drinking water source contaminated with faeces.](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/drinking-water) The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) are a set of 17 universally adopted goals outlining [a shared blueprint for peace and prosperity for people and the planet, now and into the future](https://sdgs.un.org/goals). Goal 6 aims to [Ensure availability and sustainable management of water and sanitation for all](https://sdgs.un.org/goals/goal6). <i> How do we improve the situation? </i>

As discussed earlier in this blog, often natural systems are quite good at providing clean drinking water to support life. We should therefore aim to protect and restore ecosystems as much as possible and recover their services. Did you know the total value of ecosystem services globally is estimated to be [US$33 trillion](https://www.nature.com/articles/387253a0). However, it's not as if the people impacted by water quality issues are the ones maliciously contaminating their water supply. That's why goal 17, [Strengthen the means of implementation and revitalize the Global Partnership for Sustainable Development](https://sdgs.un.org/goals/goal17), is so important.

Another crucial improvement to be made is to [be more efficient with water, especially for farming](https://www.unep.org/news-and-stories/story/global-water-shortages-are-looming-here-what-can-be-done-about-them). A significant amount of water, space, energy, and emissions are required to rear livestock for human consumption when only an estimated [10% of energy is transferred into the next level in the food chain](https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/energy-transfer-ecosystems/). A vegetarian diet would reduce these taxes and likely benefit developing and least developed countries, even if the savings are not necessarily associated with where the shortages are.

Potentially a more likely solution could be within [exploiting unconventional water sources](https://www.unep.org/news-and-stories/story/global-water-shortages-are-looming-here-what-can-be-done-about-them). Although not discussed in this blog, many developed countries have the technology and experience required to treat and reuse wastewater. Capturing part of this lost water could materially save other water sources being depleted too rapidly. For instance, if treated water can be used in agricultural irrigation, rivers may be less relied on and more water will reach receptors downstream. Likewise, water capture and storage can save energy and effort on cleaning water (depending on circumstances) while being a more trusted (even if less reliable) source. 

Water is not truly a scarce resource. There should, and could easily, be more water available than we have use for. As technology improves, this becomes truer and truer.
