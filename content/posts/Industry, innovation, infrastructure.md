Something I find interesting is the idea that growth is so often the primary indicator of success. The idea that problems go away if we have more. More anything. More everything. 

This is often a reasonable indicator, having more of something probably means you don't have to compromise on how you use it, and definitely means you're less likely to run out. 

But there is a significant cost in having more, especially when there isn't naturally enough. 

The two SDGs linked in this week's blog are SDG 9 [Build resilient infrastructure, promote inclusive and sustainable industrialization and foster innovation](https://sdgs.un.org/goals/goal9), and SDG 13 [Take urgent action to combat climate change and its impacts](https://sdgs.un.org/goals/goal13). Now, I'm not saying that these goals are inherently in opposition to each other; however, I think it is also important to include SDG 12 [Ensure sustainable consumption and production patterns](https://sdgs.un.org/goals/goal12) in this discussion. Let's discuss this with a case study.

In February 2026 the US launched [Operation Epic Fury](https://www.state.gov/releases/office-of-the-legal-adviser/2026/04/operation-epic-fury-and-international-law) against the Iranian Regime, subsequently disrupting the global oil supply chain through Iran's closure of the Hormuz Strait. In Australia, this materialised through [panic-buying at retail outlets](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-03-22/fuel-prices-albanese-government-political-risks-talking-points/106474840) and the federal government's security strategy to [underwrite private fuel cargo purchases](https://www.sbs.com.au/news/article/albanese-has-announced-new-fuel-powers-what-are-they-and-why-do-they-matter/06ws6ogrz) in order to ensure fuel entering Australia continues. 

There has been [criticism of Australia's fuel security since before the beginning of the war](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-01-07/australia-fuel-security-falling-short-foi-war-game-report/104745210), however, I think there are aspects not being discussed enough. 

Do you know anyone who has materially changed their behaviour, or otherwise been affected by the war? Sure prices are higher and people are not happy when they have to fill up, but the government cut the fuel excise to reduce the impacts, and I can think of few people who have actually had to make meaningful compromise due to the change in the economic environment. This, I believe, is really disappointing when considering how other countries have been affected. Less than a month after the beginning of the war, the [Philippines declared a national energy emergency](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c3ex8ez3717o) and has seen a [significant decrease in wages for service providers](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c1450zj6n48o). Similarly, Sri Lanka temporarily [declared Wednesdays public holidays](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c4g5n58rlnzo) in an attempt to conserve fuel. 

I'm not ignorant of the fact that other states made [public transport more accessible](https://transport.vic.gov.au/news-and-resources/news/free-public-transport-across-victoria) for a period to decrease the number of cars on the road; however, [Australia currently has more fuel in reserve than before the beginning of the war](https://budget.gov.au/content/01-fuel-supply-and-security.htm). To me, this just doesn't feel right. We were caught without resilient infrastructure and did not change our consumption patterns when they became unsustainable. Instead, we just paid more to remove ourselves from the problem. 

This idea flows into the issue of water and wastewater infrastructure. [Our demand for water will be greater than our established resources due to population growth, economic growth, and climate change](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/our-water/perths-water-supply/how-our-water-sources-have-changed). As with oil, we are increasingly throwing money at the problem rather than considering how efficient the status quo is, treating the problem as a financial one rather than behavioural.

An alternative pathway could be derived from the idea of degrowth, ["[the] reduction in the use of resources, both on the production and the consumption side for the purpose of actually preserving the thing that is most foundational to our collective lives together."](https://unswcentreforideas.com/article/degrowth) which challenges the assumption that resilience comes from having more - more reserves, more imports, more consumption. Instead, degrowth asks if resilience can be achieved by needing less.

If we look at responding to the issues mentioned in this post so far through the lens of degrowth, we are much less likely to just throw money at the problems, and more likely to make fundamental changes in efficiency that reduce the issue. 

Behavioural changes can include shorter showers, switching to native gardens, and walkable cities that reduce water-intensive urban heat. Technologies that could be implemented include greywater reuse systems, higher efficiency appliances, and dry toilets like incinerator or compost toilets which could eliminate the blackwater stream entirely. 

The greatest single water use goes to [gardens at 36%](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/help-and-advice/waterwise/garden/how-much-water-does-my-garden-need). An efficiency-driven solution in the garden is to plant native species that need watering much less frequently and can largely [survive without water at all](https://kineticlandscaping.com.au/home-landscaping-ideas/). Additionally, the household greywater stream can be use to water the garden as needed. 

Should all of this be achieved, household water use in new developments could be as much as halved without significant compromise. Obviously redesigning waste streams is non-trivial and unachievable for most households at the moment, but through natural redevelopment, houses can become increasingly efficient. 

The model of resilience that Australia understands is centred around our "rich country privilege". We continue to buy our way out of the problems others or we ourselves have created. So far it has generally worked, as we have out-competed others for oil, and nature for water. It will probably continue to work, but the [cost of living may continue to increase](https://www.abs.gov.au/media-centre/media-releases/living-costs-increase-across-all-household-types). It is constantly being said in class that water is one of Australia's most risk-averse industries, yet we still source a significant amount of water from aquifers and dams despite ecosystems relying on them and the ever-drying climate. Additionally, these sources are currently supported by only two desalination plants but at great expense. 

Relating this back to the SDGs, I think it's unclear whether this meets the objectives of goal 12. What defines sustainable consumption? Is the fact that we plan to sustain it enough? I don't think so. Regarding goal 13, one could argue that our current method - switching reliance to desalination rather than groundwater and catchments is beneficial to groundwater-dependant ecosystems, however this comes at a huge energy intensity and cost which is being met by renewable generation offsets. I am skeptical of the claim that desalination plants are carbon neutral when it is still connected to the rest of the grid, and think it is only as green as the average of all SWISS generation. To goal 9, I think our infrastructure is largely resilient, but our behaviour is not, and neither is innovative.
