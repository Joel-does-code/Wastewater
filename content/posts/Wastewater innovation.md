Perth's history has been marked by water scarcity issues from its earlier colonisation. Water restrictions have been periodically implemented to reduce water use through summer conditions [for over 100 years](https://miwer.org/2019/12/history-of-water-use-in-the-perth-bunbury-region-2/). 

The early reliance on shallow bores, drains and few storage tanks proved insufficient, and expansion of larger reservoirs, with Victoria Damm [(1891)](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/about-us/visiting-our-dams), Mundaring Weir [(1903)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mundaring_Weir), Churman Brook dam [(1920s)](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/about-us/visiting-our-dams), and Canning Dam [(1940)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canning_Dam) (amongst others), supplying water for a growing population, propped by the water pipeline to Kalgoorlie and the subsequent gold rush it enabled.

[In the 60's, 88 % of Perth's water supplied by these dams, with the remaining 12 % sourced from aquifers. By the 80's this was becoming unsustainable, with the share supplied by dams reducing to 65 %](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/our-water/perths-water-supply/how-our-water-sources-have-changed). 

If you were faced with a water environment which could no longer sustain life the way it had for living memory, what would you do? 

Drill, baby, drill!

At least that had been the answer to this point.

Was it innovative? 

Not particularly. Wells had been used by civilisations for [at least 8,000 years](https://www.hydrologica.com/post/the-history-evolution-of-wells), with artesian bores, i.e., deep wells drilled into a confined aquifer and thus providing water without a pump, used [since 1126](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artesian_well).

In Perth the first artesian bores were [dug in 1870](https://miwer.org/2019/12/history-of-water-use-in-the-perth-bunbury-region-2/), and fair enough too right? I mean the water <i>wants</i> to be extracted? Why else would it flow so readily?

For a time, this worked well (pun intended). However, the continually-drying climate really messed with everything. The reliance on groundwater ballooned, with [40% of scheme drinking water](https://begroundwaterwise.wa.gov.au/most-of-us-rely-on-groundwater/) part of the [12,000 active licenses to extract more than four trillion litres of water every year](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-06-11/wa-groundwater-extracted-without-proper-oversight-audit/105404636). This does not include private bores, a further [130,000 of which draw water from the superficial aquifer across Perth](https://library.dbca.wa.gov.au/Journals/080485/080485-12.pdf). But where across Perth? It's hard to say. [Private bores do not need to be reported to any authority](https://www.wa.gov.au/service/building-utilities-and-essential-services/water-supply/garden-bores-frequently-asked-questions). 

The problem with this is that constant water withdrawal has outpaced natural replenishment; [water levels have seen a decline over the past 40 years](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/about-us/latest-updates/august-2021/groundwater-isnt-being-replenished-as-it-once-was-affecting-local-wildlife). 

Local wetlands and bushland that depend on groundwater (GDEs), e.g., Perth's Banksia Woodlands, are consequently unable to access water like they once did. These systems can go dry, in the case of lakes / wetlands in summer, or face heat stress and die in the case of bushland / vegetation. 

As a results, whole ecosystems, including [Ramsar-listed](https://www.dcceew.gov.au/water/wetlands/australian-ramsar-wetlands) wetlands supporting migratory species, are affected by our groundwater management and withdrawal habits. 

We need to get innovative.

And we have, somewhat.

Perth uses strategic [groundwater replenishment to offset some of its extraction](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/our-water/groundwater/groundwater-replenishment). According to the Water Corporation, this is a success as it has [achieved over 100 billion litres of recharge](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/our-water/groundwater/groundwater-replenishment). 

However, this comes at an extreme cost [($422M)](https://www.awa.asn.au/resources/latest-news/australian-exemplars-of-sustainable-economic-managed-aquifer-recharge), and as mentioned, credits the Water Corporation to withdraw more water elsewhere.
