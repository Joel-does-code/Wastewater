Innovative Regulation

October 14, 2026 · 9 min

A history of Perth

Perth’s history has been marked by water scarcity issues from its earlier colonisation. Water restrictions have been periodically implemented to reduce water use through summer conditions for over 100 years.

The early reliance on shallow bores, drains and few storage tanks proved insufficient, and expansion of larger reservoirs, with Victoria Dam (1891), Mundaring Weir (1903), Churchman Brook Dam (1920s), and Canning Dam (1940) (amongst others), supplying water for a growing population, propped by the water pipeline to Kalgoorlie and the subsequent gold rush it enabled.

In the 1960s, 88% of Perth’s water was supplied by these dams, with the remaining 12% sourced from aquifers. By the 1980s, this was becoming unsustainable, with the share supplied by dams reducing to 65%.

If you were faced with a water environment which could no longer sustain life the way it had for living memory, what would you do?

Drill, baby, drill!

Wells had been used by civilisations for at least 8,000 years, with artesian bores, that is, deep wells drilled into a confined aquifer and thus providing water without a pump, used since 1126.

In Perth the first artesian bores were dug in 1870, and fair enough too right? I mean the water wants to be extracted? Why else would it flow so readily?

For a time, this worked well (pun intended). However, the continually-drying climate really messed with everything. The reliance on groundwater ballooned, with 40% of scheme drinking water part of the 12,000 active licenses to extract more than four trillion litres of water every year. This does not include private bores, a further 130,000 of which draw water from the superficial aquifer across Perth. But where across Perth? It’s hard to say. Private bores do not need to be reported to any authority.

The problem with this is that constant water withdrawal has outpaced natural replenishment; water levels have seen a decline over the past 40 years.

Local wetlands and bushland that depend on groundwater (GDEs), for example, Perth’s Banksia Woodlands, are consequently unable to access water like they once did. These systems can go dry, in the case of lakes / wetlands in summer, or face heat stress and die in the case of bushland / vegetation.

As a results, whole ecosystems, including Ramsar-listed wetlands supporting migratory species, are affected by our groundwater management and withdrawal habits.

We needed to change approach.

In 2017, the Water Corporation began groundwater replenishment into our deep aquifers.

Since then, Perth has used strategic groundwater replenishment to offset some of its extraction. According to the Water Corporation, it has achieved over 100 billion litres of recharge. And sure, in some respects, it is working as well as it could ever hope to.

But the success of this programme is fundamentally compromised. Under this strategy, residential wastewater is treated to a drinking standard before it is released into the aquifer. Meanwhile, non-drinking-quality water is extracted downstream from the same aquifer, then processed and treated before being added to Perth’s water supply.

This comes at an extreme cost, with ($422M, spent on the two stages of the Craigie development). The only reason this is normalised is due to managing public perception; people resist the idea of drinking recycled water.

So, if you accept that recycled water is undrinkable, it is probably fair to call Perth a global success story of Managed Aquifer Recharge (MAR). Our regulators have expressed concern over GDEs and have limited, and now reversed, the trend of groundwater extraction.

But our water demand is forecast to increase by between 1 and 1.6 percent per year to 2050, so we needed other options. In 2006 the Water Corporation introduced its first desalination plant, in 2011, we opened our second, and now there’s another scheduled for opening in 2028.

Again, this represents a success if you hold that the population can’t adapt and substantially reduced per-capita water usage.

It is probably unreasonable to say this is a regulatory failure - they have achieved exactly what they needed to: the plentiful supply of water. Other municipalities look to this system to help solve their own crisis; in 2017, Cape Town sought learnings from Perth to prevent the feared “day zero”. But it presents a pattern in our regulatory environment which is certainly not optimal. Essentially, we just throw money at problems to make them go away: new dams, new bores, and new desal. Solving the symptoms, but not the problem. In fairness, there have been waterwise campaigns, but these are opt-in.

On balance, we’re going to consider this a regulatory success. It has reacted to major issues and concerns in a timely fashion, compensated for natural systems where necessary, globally pioneered technologies, and remained cautious to public perception and trust.

But that’s just Perth.

Where regulation has failed

If we zoom out, just a little bit, this same regulatory framework has failed broader parts of WA.

In 2014, the Western Australian government proposed to close more than 100 remote communities due to the challenges faced in servicing them.

Remote Aboriginal communities often have small populations, limited budget allocations, and are serviced remotely through regional service providers.

In the Aboriginal Communities Water Services Drinking Water Quality Annual Report (2024-25), concentrations of E. coli and thermophilic Naegleria sometimes exceeded guideline limits, making the water non-compliant. Aesthetic water quality guidelines are also sometimes exceeded for these communities, with discrepancies described as “characteristic of the source supplying this locality” - this is also true for some non-Aboriginal regional localities. Several communities also operate non-potable systems with restrictions allowing water to be used for washing but not drinking.

Currently, we are in stage two of the three-stage, 13-year transfer and action plan, with the Water Corporation only taking over the responsibility of supplying these communities in 2023.

Historically, it was worse. Some communities faced notices to boil water before use for extended periods. Water supplies operated under a “fit-for-purpose” model, meaning water was usable but not necessarily drinkable. Further, infrastructure was often poorly maintained with no built-in redundancy, meaning any failures led to a loss of capacity.

Specifically, this was because Aboriginal communities were governed under a separate service model run by the Department of Communities, and not the Water Corporation. This meant that the policy architecture did not regulate drinking water services beyond classified towns under the Water Services Act. As communities were not recognised, there was no mechanism that required water to meet Australian Drinking Water Guidelines, and Public Drinking Water Source Areas were not prescribed for them in the Country Areas Water Supply Act.

Aboriginal communities faced a different issue to Perth: water was available but not safe. Until recently, none of the government’s policy initiatives applied to the problems faced by these communities.

This demonstrates how the WA legal landscape has treated different cohorts very differently.

Through this blog, we have reflected on how readily available funds become for protecting the water supply of Perth. We have seen policy adapt to overcome potential supply issues and respond to public concern regarding the reuse of wastewater.

We then looked at what this meant for remote WA communities, which, for the most part, was not much. Poor water quality has marred public health outcomes for Aboriginal people for decades, and although initiatives like the Closing the Gap Report and the transferal of responsibility of water services to the Water Corporation are helping, there remains a long way to go.

Innovative regulation elsewhere

Let’s zoom out further to New Zealand. We can see that other jurisdictions have approached water regulation in a fundamentally different way. Māori values and guardianship flow (again, pun intended) into national water law through Te Mana o te Wai. This recognises the fundamental importance of water and how protecting the health of freshwater consequently protects the health and well-being of the wider environment. Under this framework is Taumata Arowai, a drinking water regulator created after the Havelock North contamination crisis.

This has led to co-governance arrangements where Māori and government jointly manage rivers, lakes, and catchments, and has prioritised the drinking water needs of people before any other use. In one case, a river was granted legal personhood as a mechanism to protect it.

While there is not perfect parity between urban vs rural communities, all drinking water suppliers (in all locations) fall under this framework. Mandatory drinking water standards, source protection plans, and risk management plans apply to all communities, with all being monitored and audited.

So, what can we learn?

The most likely way to achieve equity is to have Indigenous communities inside the regulatory system rather than being serviced by it. If to be loved is to be known, then so is to be treated equally.

Protecting water sources is as important as treating water. The health of the water systems should reasonably (and majorly) contribute to environmental decisions before economics do. WA could improve how it does this with respect to handling of private extraction licenses for groundwater. In many areas, GDEs are or are forecast to struggle, all the while, private companies extract vast amounts of water.

Small communities should be afforded greater statutory protections and be fully incorporated into the regulatory system.

What is stopping us from doing this?

Appetite. Let’s not act like WA isn’t a rich state, we haven’t had a state budget deficit since FY18. Further, the money that we’ve spent (arguably wasted) on pumping drinking water into the ground could well have been spent on equity initiatives like those described for New Zealand.

No, it’s not really that simple, but it also shouldn’t be much more difficult.

In 2023, WA shelved a water reform bill that was set to streamline and ease legal process surrounding a system that was “nationally notorious for its inadequacy.”. However, the bill was sunk due to opposition drawn from mining and farming industry groups for fears of “embedding Indigenous control over water”.

Instead, we continue to rely on decades-old legislation. But it works for most, right?

Where do things stand with our global commitments?

Australia, like most other countries, has signed on to the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). These are often considered aspirational, but I think it’s more appropriate to treat them as a wake-up call. They describe unequivocally good things: no poverty, zero hunger, good health and wellbeing clean water and sanitation; yet we (Australia, most of the world) manage not to fulfill them.

We’ve shown: we still don’t have complete access to clean water and sanitation (falling short of goal 6) which can lead to adverse health outcomes (falling short of goal 3); fundamental inequalities exist in our regulation framework today (falling short of goal 10); we’ve made our cities and communities unsustainable, having to compensate with non-traditional water sources (falling short of goal 11); and we use these non-traditional water sources heavily, without materially fixing the problem of water scarcity, only patching it (falling short of goal 12).

Regulations are hard to fix; there is a lot of inertia associated with the status-quo. But there are examples we can learn from.
